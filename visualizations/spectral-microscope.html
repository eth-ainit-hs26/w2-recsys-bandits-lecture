The table from Demo 1 has 336 cells: 181 likes and dislikes, the rest no clear opinion. Behind them sit a few hidden taste dimensions. How many does the computer need to redraw the whole table? Move the slider and watch the copy on the right fill in.

What is a dimension?

One hidden taste question that the computer finds by itself in the ratings.

How the copy uses it

Every reader and every book gets a score on the question. The copy multiplies the two. For dimension 1:

Alice high× Potter high→ liked
Alice high× Kant low→ disliked
Eleni zero× any book→ blank

With more dimensions, their products are added together.

How much each dimension adds

What's happening?

The computer looks for the taste dimensions that explain the most ratings, biggest first. Dimension 1 separates Commuters (light fiction) from Scholars (demanding nonfiction). Dimension 2 separates Literary readers from Practical ones. With these two, the copy gets 180 of the 181 ratings right. Dimensions 3 and 4 only add details about two unusual readers, Samir and Ursula.

Why this matters: a recommender does not need to keep the whole table, only each reader's and each book's scores. Two scores each make 21 × 2 + 16 × 2 = 74 numbers instead of 336 cells, and already get 180 of the 181 ratings right. The exact table needs all four dimensions, 148 numbers. Keeping only the big dimensions is the trade a recommender makes: far less to store, almost nothing lost.

ΣShow the mathHide the math

The method is the singular value decomposition (SVD). It writes the ratings table A as a sum of layers, one per dimension, biggest first. The copy from k dimensions keeps the first k layers:

Ak=σ1u1v1⊤+σ2u2v2⊤ +…+σkukvk⊤ =UkΣkVk⊤

ur holds the 21 reader scores on dimension r and vr the 16 book scores; the singular value σr says how much that dimension matters. The bars above show σr2 as a share of the total.
