% ===== Frame 1. Friday 08:00, after the nearest neighbours: Airbnb, 2018 =====
% Say: A first case for what we just did in the video store. When a guest looks at a home on Airbnb, a
% row of similar homes appears beside it. Airbnb's engineers gave each home a list of 32
% numbers, learned from more than 800 million search sessions: homes that the same guest clicked in
% one session end up with similar numbers. The similar homes are the 12 nearest neighbours of the home
% on the screen, among the homes in the same market and free on the same dates. The A/B test compared
% this with the old method, which ran the search ranking for the same place and kept the homes of the
% same price range and type. With the nearest neighbours the row had a 21 percent higher click rate,
% and 4.9 percent more guests found there the home they finally booked. That row matters: search and
% similar homes together bring 99 percent of Airbnb's bookings. What it cost: a new home has no sessions
% yet, so its numbers are the average of the 3 closest homes of the same type and price. And 32 numbers
% was a trade-off between the offline results and the memory of the search machines, which hold every
% vector. What they decided: learn from the home the guest finally booked as well as from the clicks,
% and launch only after offline tests and an online experiment. The source is Airbnb's engineering blog
% of March 2018; the paper appeared at KDD the same year.
\begin{frame}{Airbnb, 2018: similar homes are nearest neighbours}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 08:00, the video store}
      {Each home is a list of \abDims~numbers, learned from over \abSessions~million search sessions.
       Similar homes: its \abK~nearest neighbours.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B test}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\abCtr\%}{click rate of the row of similar homes}
    \cardnumber{2}{+\abFound\%}{guests who found their booking here}
    \cardcost{A new home has no sessions yet: its numbers are the average of \abCold~similar homes nearby.}
    \carddecided{Airbnb}{Learn from the homes guests booked as well as clicked; launch after an online test.}
    \cardsource{Grbovic and others (Airbnb), Airbnb Tech Blog, 13 March 2018. Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 2. Friday 10:00, after matrix factorization: the Netflix Prize, 2006 to 2009 =====
% Say: The second case is the most famous contest in this field. In October 2006 Netflix published 100
% million ratings, from 480,000 customers, and offered one million dollars to whoever guessed hidden
% ratings 10 percent better than its own system did at the start. Its own system, Cinematch, was a
% nearest neighbour method: films similar by their ratings, our case of 08:00. 51,051 contestants from
% 186 countries registered. In July 2009 a team of seven reached 10.06 percent better than Cinematch,
% measured on ratings that Netflix kept hidden, and won on 21 September. By 2008 their winning entries
% held more than 100 models, most of them matrix factorizations: the profiles of this morning, one row
% per customer, one row per film; the final blend of 2009 held hundreds. What it cost: the ratings were
% anonymous, but two researchers matched them to ratings that people had posted publicly on IMDb. Of
% about 50 IMDb users they tried, they found two in the Netflix data, and for one of them the films
% suggested political and religious views. The authors call it a proof of concept. What Netflix decided:
% it had announced a second contest; the US Federal Trade Commission raised privacy concerns and a law
% firm filed a lawsuit. In March 2010 Netflix cancelled the contest, settled the lawsuit, and told the
% FTC that any future data would go only to researchers who sign a contract limiting its use. A contest
% is one way to buy research: the winners had to grant Netflix a non-exclusive licence to their methods.
\begin{frame}{Netflix, 2009: the prize went to factorization}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 10:00, the matchmaker}
      {Netflix shared \nfRatings~million ratings and offered \$\nfPrize~million for guesses
       \nfTarget\% better than its own system.}
    \cardresult{Result, on hidden ratings}
    \cardnumber{1}{\nfWin\%}{better than Netflix's own system}
    \cardnumber{2}{\nfModels+}{models by 2008, most factorizations}
    \cardcost{Researchers matched public IMDb ratings to the anonymous data: \nsFound~of~about~\nsSample~IMDb
      users identified.}
    \carddecided{Netflix}{After FTC privacy concerns and a lawsuit, cancel Netflix Prize 2 (March 2010).}
    \cardsource{Netflix Prize rules (2006) and results (2009). Koren, Bell, Volinsky, IEEE Computer 2009.
      Narayanan and Shmatikov, IEEE S\&P 2008. Netflix blog and FTC letter, 12 March 2010.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 3. Friday 12:00, after the bandits: Adyen, how to send each payment, 2020 to 2025 =====
% Say: The third case comes from payments. Adyen processes card payments for shops and services. A
% payment can go to the customer's bank in several ways: with 3D Secure or without, as an instant retry,
% with the message formatted another way. After the filters for contracts, card network rules and risk,
% a payment typically has 2 to 15 such options. A model predicts which option gets the payment approved,
% and the rule is the one from this lecture, epsilon-greedy: the best guess almost always, a random
% option now and then. Adyen then tested a smarter way to explore, which tries the options that are
% nearly as good more often. In the main comparison each version got 5 percent of the live traffic for
% 4 weeks. Result: the best smarter version approved 0.1 percent more payments than the epsilon-greedy
% bandit already running. That sounds like nothing, but at Adyen's volume it is, by their estimate, 9
% million more approved payments a year. The paper does not say whether Adyen switched. What it cost:
% every explored payment risks a decline. And a surprise: smarter exploring left fewer declines in the
% data, and the next model trained on that data did 0.2 percent worse; the authors attribute the drop
% to the missing declines. What Adyen decided: rules come first. Merchant contracts and card network
% rules remove options before the bandit picks, and risky options are pruned too. By November 2020 the
% bandit had replaced A/B tests per merchant and bank: with more payment features the test groups got
% too small, and every test had to be rerun when the world changed. The models are retrained every 7
% days.
\begin{frame}{Adyen, 2025: a better bandit approves more payments}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 12:00, the bandits}
      {Most payments can reach the bank in \adOptMin~to~\adOptMax~ways. Epsilon-greedy picks the best
       bet, and now and then another.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B test}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\adUplift\%}{approval rate, smarter exploring}
    \cardnumber{2}{\adExtra M}{more approvals a year, estimated}
    \cardcost{Exploring risks declines. Smarter exploring left fewer declines in the data: the next model
      did \adRegress\% worse.}
    \carddecided{Adyen}{Rules first: contracts and card network rules remove options before the bandit
      picks.}
    \cardsource{Vangara and Egg (Adyen), OARS workshop, KDD 2025. van Rooijen (Adyen), Adyen Knowledge Hub,
      2~November 2020.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 4. Friday 14:00, after gradient descent: what Netflix put into production, 2012 =====
% Say: Back to Netflix, three years on. A year into the prize, the leading team had blended 107 models,
% after more than 2,000 hours of work, and reached 8.43 percent; they gave Netflix the code. The
% factorizations of the prize learn by gradient descent, this afternoon's method: one contestant
% published it in December 2006, with a step size of 0.001, and a pass over all 100 million ratings took
% his laptop about 7.5 seconds. Netflix took the two strongest of the 107 models into production: a
% matrix factorization and a neural network called a restricted Boltzmann machine. What it cost: they
% were built for 100 million ratings, Netflix had more than 5 billion, and they had to keep learning as
% members added ratings. And the winning blend of 2009, hundreds of models for 10.06 percent? Netflix
% tested some of the newer methods offline: the gains it measured did not seem to justify the
% engineering, and by then its focus had moved on, one reason being the move to streaming. Netflix also
% says an offline score only decides what to test next; the A/B test decides. The source is Netflix's engineering blog,
% April 2012.
\begin{frame}{Netflix, 2012: the winning blend did not ship}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 14:00, gradient descent}
      {Netflix put the two strongest prize models into production: a matrix factorization and a
       neural network.}
    \cardresult{Result, on hidden ratings}
    \cardnumber{1}{\nfPPGain\%}{\nfPPModels~models, 2007}
