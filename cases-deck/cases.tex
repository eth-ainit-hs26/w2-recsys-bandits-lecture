% ===== Frame 1. Friday 08:00, after the nearest neighbours: Airbnb, 2018 =====
% Say: A first case for what we just did in the video store. When a guest looks at a home on Airbnb, a
% row of similar homes appears beside it. Airbnb's engineers gave each home a list of 32
% numbers, learned from more than 800 million search sessions: homes that the same guest clicked in
% one session end up with similar numbers. The similar homes are the 12 nearest neighbours of the home
% on the screen, among the homes free on the same dates. In an A/B test, the row with this method got
% 21 percent more clicks, and 4.9 percent more guests found there the home they finally booked. That
% row matters: search and similar homes together bring 99 percent of Airbnb's bookings. What it cost: a
% new home has no sessions yet, so its numbers are the average of the 3 closest homes of the same type
% and price. And 32 numbers was a choice: more numbers fitted better offline, but every vector has to
% sit in the memory of the search machines. What they decided: learn from the home the guest finally
% booked as well as from the clicks, and launch only after offline tests and an online experiment. The
% source is Airbnb's engineering blog of March 2018; the paper appeared at KDD the same year.
\begin{frame}{Airbnb, 2018: similar homes are nearest neighbours}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 08:00, the video store}
      {Each home is a list of \abDims~numbers, learned from \abSessions~million search sessions.
       Similar homes: its \abK~nearest neighbours.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B test}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\abCtr\%}{click rate of the row of similar homes}
    \cardnumber{2}{+\abFound\%}{bookings discovered in this row}
    \cardcost{A new home has no sessions yet: its numbers are the average of \abCold~similar homes nearby.}
    \carddecided{Airbnb}{Learn from the homes guests booked as well as clicked; launch after an online test.}
    \cardsource{Grbovic and others (Airbnb), Airbnb Tech Blog, 13 March 2018. Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 2. Friday 10:00, after matrix factorization: the Netflix Prize, 2006 to 2009 =====
% Say: The second case is the most famous contest in this field. In October 2006 Netflix published 100
% million ratings, from 480,000 customers, and offered one million dollars to whoever guessed hidden
% ratings 10 percent better than its own system. Its own system, Cinematch, was a nearest neighbour
% method: films similar by their ratings, our case of 08:00. 51,051 people from 186 countries entered.
% In July 2009 a team of seven reached 10.06 percent, measured on ratings that Netflix kept hidden, and
% won on 21 September. The winning entries held more than 100 models, most of them matrix
% factorizations: the profiles of this morning, one row per customer, one row per film. What it cost:
% the ratings were anonymous, but two researchers matched them to ratings that people had posted
% publicly on IMDb, and identified customers, with what their films suggested about their politics.
% What Netflix decided: it had announced a second prize; after the US Federal Trade Commission asked
% about privacy and a law firm filed a lawsuit, Netflix cancelled it in March 2010. A contest is one way
% to buy research: the winners had to license their methods to Netflix.
\begin{frame}{Netflix, 2009: the prize went to factorization}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 10:00, the matchmaker}
      {Netflix shared \nfRatings~million ratings and offered \$\nfPrize~million for guesses
       \nfTarget\% better than its own system.}
    \cardresult{Result, on hidden ratings}
    \cardnumber{1}{\nfWin\%}{better than Netflix's own system}
    \cardnumber{2}{\nfModels+}{models, most of them factorizations}
    \cardcost{Researchers matched the anonymous ratings to public ratings on IMDb and identified customers.}
    \carddecided{Netflix}{After a question from the FTC and a lawsuit, cancel the second prize (March 2010).}
    \cardsource{Netflix Prize rules (2006) and results (2009). Koren, Bell, Volinsky, IEEE Computer 2009.
      Narayanan and Shmatikov, IEEE S\&P 2008. Netflix blog, 12 March 2010.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 3. Friday 12:00, after the bandits: Adyen, how to send each payment, 2020 to 2025 =====
% Say: The third case comes from payments. Adyen processes card payments for shops and services. A
% payment can go to the customer's bank in several ways: with 3D Secure or without, as an instant retry,
% with the message formatted another way. Each payment has 2 to 15 such options. A model predicts which
% option gets the payment approved, and the rule is the one from this lecture, epsilon-greedy: the best
% guess almost always, a random option now and then. Adyen then tested a smarter way to explore,
% which tries the options that are nearly as good more often. Each version got 5 percent of the live
% traffic for 4 weeks. Result: 0.1 percent more approved payments. That sounds like nothing, but at
% Adyen's volume it is, by their estimate, 9 million more approved payments a year. What it cost: every
% explored payment risks a decline. And a surprise: smarter exploring left fewer declines in the data,
% and the next model trained on that data scored 0.2 percent lower; the authors link the two. What Adyen decided: rules come first.
% Merchant contracts and card network rules remove options before the bandit picks, and risky options
% are pruned too. In 2020 the bandit replaced A/B tests per merchant and bank, whose samples were too
% small; the models are retrained every 7 days.
\begin{frame}{Adyen, 2025: a bandit gets more payments approved}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 12:00, the bandits}
      {Each payment can reach the bank in \adOptMin~to \adOptMax~ways. Epsilon-greedy picks the best
       bet, and now and then another.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B test}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\adUplift\%}{approval rate, smarter exploring}
    \cardnumber{2}{\adExtra M}{more approvals a year, estimated}
    \cardcost{Exploring risks declines. Smarter exploring left fewer declines in the data: the next model
      scored \adRegress\% lower.}
    \carddecided{Adyen}{Rules first: contracts and card network rules remove options before the bandit
      picks.}
    \cardsource{Vangara and Egg (Adyen), OARS workshop, KDD 2025. van Rooijen (Adyen), Adyen Knowledge Hub,
      2 November 2020.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 4. Friday 14:00, after gradient descent: what Netflix put into production, 2012 =====
% Say: Back to Netflix, three years on. A year into the prize, the leading team had blended 107 models,
% after more than 2,000 hours of work, and reached 8.43 percent; they gave Netflix the code. The
% factorizations of the prize learn by gradient descent, this afternoon's method: one contestant published it in December
% 2006, with a step size of 0.001, and a pass over all 100 million ratings took his laptop 7.5 seconds.
% Netflix took the two strongest of the 107 models, a matrix factorization and a neural network, into
% production. What it cost: they were built for 100 million ratings, Netflix had more than 5 billion, and
% they had to keep learning as members added ratings. And the winning blend of 2009, hundreds of models
% for 10.06 percent? Netflix measured it offline and did not ship it: in its words, the additional
% accuracy did not seem to justify the engineering effort. Netflix also says an offline score only
% decides what to test next; the A/B test decides. The source is Netflix's engineering blog, April 2012.
\begin{frame}{Netflix, 2012: the winning blend did not ship}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 14:00, gradient descent}

       neural network.}
    \cardresult{Result, on hidden ratings}
    \cardnumber{1}{\nfPPGain\%}{\nfPPModels~models, 2007}
    \cardnumber{2}{\nfWin\%}{hundreds of models, 2009}
    \cardcost{Built for \nfBuiltFor~million ratings, the models had to learn from more than \nfHad~billion,
      and keep learning as members rated.}
