% ===== Frame 1. Friday 08:00, after the nearest neighbours: Etsy, search candidates by inner product, 2024 =====
% Say: A first case for what we just did in the video store, from Etsy, the marketplace for handmade and
% vintage goods, with more than 100 million items. When you type a search, Etsy first shortlists several
% thousand products. Historically, keyword search did that. Since this work part of the shortlist comes the
% way we did it: your search, with your context such as your location and your recent searches, becomes a list
% of numbers; every product becomes a list of numbers; and the products with the highest inner product join
% the shortlist. One trick: Etsy appends a few quality numbers to every product's list, such as its shop's
% popularity, and matching weights to the search's list, so the same inner product scores relevance and quality
% at once. In A/B tests on live traffic, the new search raised the purchase rate of searches by 5.58 percent and
% the conversion rate of the whole site by 2.63 percent. Both are relative changes, aggregated over several
% tests. What it cost: a plain version of the model lost to old keyword search on popular searches. Of the
% products people bought after such a search, it had 32 percent among its top 100 results; keyword search had
% 63 percent. Adding the person's context closed the gap. Training takes about 72 hours on 4 GPUs. What Etsy
% decided: speed first. The shortlist must come back within tens of milliseconds, so Etsy searches a compressed
% index, fetches five times more products than it needs and re-ranks them: under 4 percent of the true nearest
% products are missed, and 99 percent of the lookups take under 20 milliseconds. The source is Etsy's paper, a
% preprint of 2023 presented at a conference in 2024.
\begin{frame}{Etsy, 2024: nearest neighbours join keyword search}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 08:00, the video store}
      {Each search and each product is a list of numbers; the products nearest by inner product join the
       shortlist.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B tests, relative}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\etCvr\%}{conversion rate of the whole site}
    \cardnumber{2}{+\etOspr\%}{purchase rate of searches}
    \cardcost{On popular searches a plain version found \etHeadBase\%~of~the~products~bought;
      keyword~search~\etHeadBM\%.}
    \carddecided{Etsy}{A compressed index: under~\etLoss\% of the nearest products missed, lookups within
      \etMs~ms.}
    \cardsource{Jha, Subramaniyam, Benjamin, Taula (Etsy), IEEE FMLDS 2024; preprint arXiv, June 2023.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 2. Friday 10:00, after matrix factorization: Spotify, audiobook recommendations, 2024 =====
% Say: The second case is from 2024. When Spotify added audiobooks, it had almost nothing to learn from: at
% launch each audiobook had to be bought before it could be streamed, and a quarter of the listeners made three
% quarters of the audiobook hours. But over 70 percent of the first audiobook listeners had listened to
% podcasts. So Spotify's engineers built something close to the matchmaker: a list of numbers for every
% listener and every audiobook, here 128 numbers each, and a score that is their inner product. In the
% matchmaker we learned the numbers directly; Spotify computes them with two networks, from what people
% streamed, podcasts included, and from each book's description. The shelf "Audiobook for you" on the home
% page picks from the audiobooks whose numbers have the highest inner product with the listener's. The test:
% 11.5 million monthly users, split at random into 3 groups: the model already in production, a simpler new
% model, and this one. Result: against the production model, the rate of people starting a new audiobook rose
% by 46.8 percent, a relative change; with the simpler model it rose by 23.9 percent. What it cost: in offline
% tests, for users who had never touched an audiobook, its suggestions cover only 12 percent of the catalogue,
% against 19.3 percent for the simpler model. It is more accurate, on a narrower shelf. What Spotify decided:
% the model went into production for every eligible user, and the narrower catalogue is a trade-off to revisit
% later. The source is Spotify's paper at the Web Conference 2024.
\begin{frame}{Spotify, 2024: podcasts reveal audiobook tastes}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 10:00, the matchmaker}
      {Each listener and each audiobook is \spDims~numbers, learned from what people streamed, podcasts
       included.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B test}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\spStart\%}{listeners starting a new audiobook}
    \cardnumber{2}{\spUsers M}{monthly active users in the test}
    \cardcost{For users new to audiobooks its picks cover only \spCoverNew\% of the catalogue, against
      \spCoverPlain\% for a simpler model.}
    \carddecided{Spotify}{In production for every eligible user; the narrower catalogue to be revisited.}
    \cardsource{De Nadai and others (Spotify), Companion Proceedings of the Web Conference (WWW) 2024.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 3. Friday 12:00, after the bandits: Adyen, how to send each payment, 2025 =====
% Say: The third case comes from payments, published in 2025. Adyen processes card payments for shops
% and services. Before a payment goes to the customer's bank, Adyen can change how it is sent: the logic,
% or the data attached to the transaction. After the filters for contracts, card network rules and risk,
% a payment typically has 2 to 15 such options. A model predicts which option gets the payment approved,
% and the rule is the one from this lecture, epsilon-greedy: the best guess almost always, a random
% option now and then. Adyen then tested a smarter way to explore, which tries the options that are
% nearly as good more often. In the main comparison each version got 5 percent of the live traffic for
% 4 weeks. Result: the best smarter version approved 0.1 percent more payments than the epsilon-greedy
% bandit already running. That sounds like nothing, but at Adyen's volume it is, by their estimate, 9
% million more approved payments a year. The paper does not say whether Adyen switched. What it cost:
% every explored payment risks a decline. And a surprise: smarter exploring left fewer declines in the
% data, and the next model trained on that data did 0.2 percent worse; the authors attribute the drop
% to the missing declines. What Adyen decided: rules come first. Merchant contracts and card network
% rules remove options before the bandit picks, and risky options are pruned too. The models are
% retrained every 7 days. The explored payments have a second use: Adyen evaluates new versions on
% these logs offline, alongside its A/B tests, after one analysis found 58 percent of its A/B tests flat
% or inconclusive, more than 20 weeks of testing a year wasted.
\begin{frame}{Adyen, 2025: a better bandit approves more payments}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 12:00, the bandits}
      {Most payments can reach the bank in \adOptMin~to~\adOptMax~ways. Epsilon-greedy picks the best
       bet, and now and then another.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B test}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\adUplift\%}{approval rate, smarter exploring}
    \cardnumber{2}{\adExtra M}{more approvals a year, estimated}
    \cardcost{Exploring risks declines. Smarter exploring left fewer declines in the data: the next model
      did \adRegress\% worse.}
    \carddecided{Adyen}{Rules first: contracts and card network rules remove options before the bandit
      picks.}
    \cardsource{Vangara and Egg (Adyen), OARS workshop, KDD 2025. Egg (Adyen), Off-policy Evaluation for
      Payments at Adyen, arXiv, January 2025.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}

% ===== Frame 4. Friday 14:00, after gradient descent: LinkedIn, large ranking models, 2024 =====
% Say: The last case of the day is LinkedIn, 2024. LinkedIn, with more than 1 billion members, ranks the
% posts in your feed, the jobs it suggests and the ads it shows with models trained by gradient descent,
% this afternoon's method. The feed model has billions of parameters, and it now learns from all feed
% sessions, up from 13 percent. The training runs on several GPUs at once. In online A/B tests, the new
% models brought 0.5 percent more member sessions in the feed, about 0.9 percent more qualified job
% applications, both in job search and in job suggestions, and a 4.3 percent higher click rate on ads. All
% three are relative changes. What it cost: one training run took 70 hours; in LinkedIn's benchmarks,
% splitting the model's large tables across the GPUs brought it down to 20. And many early runs diverged, so
% the step size now grows slowly over the first half of training, where it used to grow over the first 5
% percent. What LinkedIn decided: more training steps would have helped, but every experiment would have taken
% longer. So they took bigger steps: a learning rate three times larger closed almost all of the gap. And a new
% model starts from the previous one, stays close to it, and learns only from the newest data; in their tests
% this cut training time by 96 percent. One model, for short-lived posts, is retrained every 8 hours. The
% source is LinkedIn's paper at KDD 2024.
\begin{frame}{LinkedIn, 2024: bigger steps instead of longer training}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm, y=1mm]
    \path[use as bounding box] (0,1) rectangle (130,-54);
    \cardbuilt{This weekend: Friday 14:00, gradient descent}
      {Models rank the feed, jobs and ads. The feed's has billions of parameters, trained on several GPUs.}
    \cardresult{Result, A/B tests, relative}
    \cardnumber{1}{+\liSessions\%}{member sessions in the feed}
    \cardnumber{2}{+\liAdsCtr\%}{click rate on ads}
    \cardcost{A training run took \liHoursBefore~hours; splitting the model across GPUs cut it to
      \liHoursAfter~hours. Many early runs diverged.}
    \carddecided{LinkedIn}{Longer training would slow experiments: triple the learning rate instead.}
    \cardsource{Borisyuk and others (LinkedIn), LiRank: Industrial Large Scale Ranking Models at LinkedIn,
      KDD 2024.}
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{frame}
