% ===== Frame 1. Etsy, the shortlist, and the words that do not match =====
% Say: Etsy is the online marketplace for handmade, vintage and craft items, more than 100 million of them. For
% each search it first picks a shortlist of several thousand items, and a ranking model then orders the
% shortlist. The shortlist used to come from keyword matching only: an item got in when its title or tags shared
% words with the search. That fails when buyers and sellers use different words. [click] A buyer types "mothers
% day gift"; the seller of a birth flower necklace never wrote the word gift. Etsy's paper names exactly such
% searches as common on Etsy and hard to serve from the words alone. The listing here is made up.

% ===== Frame 2. The training data =====
% Say: What did Etsy learn from? Its own search logs. Every positive action after a search, such as adding an item
% to the cart or buying it, is one row: the search words, who searched, with their location and what they
% searched or clicked before, and the item. One search can give several rows. These rows are made up, but this is
% the shape. Etsy took one month of such rows from its apps and its website: more than 150 million of them, from
% up to 30 million users. During training the model also sees other items, random ones and look-alikes, as
% examples of what the search did not want.

% ===== Frame 3. A list of numbers for every search and every item =====
% Say: From those rows Etsy trains two networks. The first reads the search together with the person: the
% words, the location, the recent searches, the shops clicked. Out comes a list of numbers. The second reads an
% item: its title, tags and category, and the searches that led to it in the past. Out comes a list of numbers
% of the same length. Here three numbers each, made up; Etsy's lists are longer. [click] Training moves the
% numbers until the inner product, multiply number by number and add, as in the video store, is high for the
% items someone added to the cart or bought after that search, and low for other items. For our search and the
% necklace it is 7.
\begin{frame}{Every search and every item becomes a list of numbers}

% ===== Frame 4. A search arrives: sort by inner product =====
% Say: Now a search arrives. [the frame opens on it] The search network turns "mothers day gift", with this
% person's context, into its list of numbers. [click] Take one item, the necklace; its numbers were computed in
% advance. The inner product: multiply number by number, then add. 4, 2 and 1, sum 7. [click] The same for every
% item, here four. [click] Sort by the inner product: the top two join the shortlist. The necklace comes first
% although it shares no word with the search. The gift wrap, which has the word gift, comes last; keyword
% matching, which still runs beside it, can bring it in. Etsy finds almost the same top among more than 100

% ===== Frame 5. Cost and value =====
% Say: The cost: training takes about 72 hours on 4 GPUs, from one month of cart adds and purchases; the item
% side also draws on a year of past searches. The value, measured in A/B tests on live traffic and aggregated
% over several tests: the conversion rate of the whole site rose by 2.63 percent, and the purchase rate of
% searches by 5.58 percent, both relative changes. [click] Was it easy? No. An offline measure: of the products
% people bought after a popular search, how many were among a version's top 100? A plain version that read only
% the search words and the item text found 32 percent; keyword search found 63 percent; the final model, with
% the person's context and the quality numbers among other things, found 72 percent.
