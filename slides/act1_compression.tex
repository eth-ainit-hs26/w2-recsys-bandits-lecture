\begin{frame}{The geometry was always there}
  \sidenote{Both axes are old library science: Dewey decimal (1876)
            gives Fiction vs.\ Nonfiction; Flesch (1948) and Lexile (1989)
            give Light vs.\ Demanding.}
  Two binary distinctions, four corners.  This is how librarians and
  educators have sorted books for a century.

  \vspace{0.8em}

  We did not impose these axes.  We \AINITemph{recovered} them ---
  from a matrix of likes and dislikes alone.

  \vspace{0.4em}

  The books told us their geometry, and we listened.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{But we only had \emph{R}}
  \medskip
  We never observed $U$ or $V$ directly.  The library's ledger has only
  $R$ --- the rating matrix, three hundred and thirty-six cells of
  symbols.

  \medskip
  Yet the matrix is the product of two skinny ones.  So the question is:

  \vspace{1.6em}

  \begin{center}
    \itshape\large
    Given $R$ alone, can we recover $U$ and $V$?
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.6em}

  This is the \AINITemph{matrix factorization} problem.  The next demo
  gives one way to solve it.
\end{frame}
