The empirical estimator funnels into the truth
One hidden coin with bias $p$. We run $K$ independent experiments in parallel, each tracking its own running average $\hat p_n$ over $n$ tosses. As $n$ grows, every trajectory is squeezed toward the true $p$ — and the width of the funnel shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$. This is the law of large numbers, made visual.

The empirical std is roughly $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n^*}$, which for fixed $p$ shrinks as $1/\sqrt{n^*}$. The fraction in the band is around 68% — Gaussian one-sigma — because the CLT kicks in fast.

The math behind the funnel

empirical estimator
$$\hat{p}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$
law of large numbers (convergence in probability)
$$\hat{p}_n \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} p$$
precise statement
$$\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\hat{p}_n - p| > \delta\bigr) < \epsilon \quad \text{for } n \text{ sufficiently large.}$$

Reading the picture. Pick any horizontal slice $\delta > 0$ around the vermillion line. For small $n$, plenty of trajectories live outside that slice. For large $n$, almost none do. That's the LLN. The blue dashed band of half-width $1/\sqrt{n}$ shows the typical scale of fluctuation — a one-line preview of the bound that powers UCB on the next slide.

Why this matters for bandits. A bandit algorithm has to decide which arm is best based on its empirical mean reward. The funnel tells us how trustworthy that empirical mean is at step $n$. UCB acts as if the truth lives somewhere inside a $1/\sqrt{n}$ band — exactly what you see here.
